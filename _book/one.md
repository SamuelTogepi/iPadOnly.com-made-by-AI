“People laugh at me because I describe the iPad as magical. You have a much more direct and intimate relationship with the Internet and media and apps and your content.” 
— Steve Jobs, D8 Conference

Humans don’t just crave social bonds; we also love to physically touch things. If you’ve spent any time glued to an iPad—especially when treating an older, legacy piece of hardware as your daily driver—think about the last time you absentmindedly poked the screen of a traditional laptop, expecting it to react like a tablet. We’ve all done it! Touching the glass directly is lightyears ahead of staring at a monitor while clicking away with a mouse. The iPad is all about touch.

Before the iPad came along, Microsoft pushed the "Tablet PC" concept—pricey, bulky early laptops that let you interact with the display. The people who bought them genuinely loved them. Even though they relied on an annoying, easily lost plastic stylus, there was something magical about writing and tapping directly on the screen that a standard desktop could never touch (pun intended). 

Long before we built our `#MainNostalgiaComputer2Go` routines, some of us dabbled with those old Tablet PCs before migrating to macOS. We missed the direct interaction of touch, even while enjoying the polish of desktop operating systems. But when the iPad arrived, that tactile feeling came roaring back a hundredfold. Steve Jobs was right: the finger really is the best stylus ever made. Suddenly, we weren't just clicking a cursor over text; we could physically *touch* our emails, swipe through articles, pinch-to-zoom on images, and spin content around. 

It created a deeply intimate relationship with our data. We weren't just looking *at* a computer; we were handling the content itself, feeling infinitely more connected than we ever did behind a keyboard and mouse. 

Developers leaned into this, crafting apps that turned gesture control into an art form. Swiping, pinching, and dragging made computing fun, intuitive, and natural. 

That’s why we completely agree with Steve Jobs—it is an entirely different, highly intimate way to compute. After years of working almost exclusively on legacy iPads using older, snappier firmware, we aren't tired of it for a second. With modern apps still honoring those foundational gesture designs, the fun hasn't stopped. There's no turning back. This intimate connection to our screens is here to stay.
